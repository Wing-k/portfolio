Hardware Usability Test

Hardware Usability Test

Designed a study protocol and conducted usability tests to uncover usability issues and redesign recommendations on the Nintendo Switch

Role
UX Researcher
Team
6 UX researchers
Duration
3 months (Mar 2025 - May 2025)
Process
User Research · Study Design · Test Questionnaire · Data Collection & Analysis · Insight Synthesis
Tools
Nintendo Switch · Camera

The problem

Bridge the gap between Nintendo's goals and what users are experiencing by conducting usability tests to collect and synthesize insights for design recommendations.

Nintendo's Goals

An intuitive and easily usable console for players of all ages and experience levels. Design of the hardware(touchscreen, Joy-Con configurations, and the dock) offers flexible and multiple forms of gaming experience.

Impacts & Outcomes

Research

· Conducted usability testing with 6 participants across 3 user groups (novice, experienced, parent) to evaluate first-time setup, controller pairing, and parental controls, uncovering critical interaction breakdowns.

· Synthesized 10+ usability issues into 4 high-priority themes, revealing that unclear system feedback and inconsistent instructions significantly increased cognitive load for novice and parent users.

· Measured task completion, error rates, time-on-task, and post-task satisfaction across task interactions, identifying where learnability and clarity failed.

Design

· Translated findings into 6+ prioritized, actionable design recommendations, including clearer onboarding cues, improved feedback states, and simplified parental control flows.

1

Dimensions of Interest

Based on user goals and Nintendo's core values, my team evaluated the Switch across seven key dimensions of interest. These served as our framework for testing and analysis:

Social Connectivity: We assessed the console's ability to foster community and shared gaming experiences.

Accessibility: We evaluated its usability for a broad range of users, considering cognitive, physical, and visual needs.

Entertainment & Satisfaction: We measured how well the console delivers an enjoyable and engaging experience, from gameplay to content richness.

Environmental Sustainability: This dimension focused on the Switch's alignment with environmentally responsible use across its lifecycle.

Safety: We looked at features like parental controls and privacy settings that protect users from online risks.

Learnability: We assessed how quickly and efficiently new users could understand and begin using the console.

Customization: We evaluated the extent to which users can personalize their gaming experience to their unique preferences.

2

Participant recruitment

User Groups & Persona

Recruited 6 participants across 3 categories: 2 novice users, 2 experienced users, 2 novice parent users. Screened for experience with Nintendo Switch.

3

Metrics

Participants logged tasks, tools, and friction moments daily via a lightweight form. Generated 340+ diary entries across the cohort.

4

Contextual inquiry sessions

16 x 90-minute sessions observing participants working live. I watched, asked "why" questions in the moment, and photographed workarounds with consent.

5

Cross-team synthesis

Two-day synthesis sprint with 4 team members. Built a full journey map covering 9 distinct workflow patterns. Identified 3 critical failure modes.

6

Executive readout

Presented findings to the C-suite with a narrative structure: what we believed, what we found, what it means for the roadmap.

Key insights

The diary study and contextual sessions painted a consistent picture. The product had been designed around individual task completion, but ops work is fundamentally about coordination and context — two things the tool didn't support well.

01

The shadow system problem

Every team had a parallel "real" system running alongside the product — Slack threads, personal spreadsheets, sticky note walls. The tool captured tasks but not decisions.

02

Context collapse

Tasks were logged without the reasoning behind them. When ownership changed hands, institutional knowledge was lost. People were completing tasks they didn't understand.

03

Status ≠ progress

The tool showed task status well, but teams needed to communicate momentum — blockers, dependencies, and confidence levels — none of which had a home in the UI.

Outcomes

Findings directly influenced the next two product planning cycles. Three new features were scoped from the research, and two existing features were deprioritised after the evidence showed they weren't addressing real pain points.

What I'd do differently

The diary study format asked too much of participants — the drop-off rate in week 2 was higher than I'd like. I'd redesign it as a voice-note based study next time: 60 seconds spoken is far lower friction than filling out a form at the end of a long day.

I'd also push harder for access to the enterprise client earlier. We got the richest signal from their team, but recruitment took 4 weeks due to procurement. Building that relationship before the study starts is worth the upfront effort.
