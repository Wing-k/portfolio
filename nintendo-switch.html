Nintendo Switch Usability Study

Designed a study protocol and conducted usability tests to uncover usability issues and redesign recommendations on the Nintendo Switch.

Role
UX Researcher
Duration
3 months
Team
6 UX Researchers
Tasks
User Research Study Design Data Collection & Analysis Synthesis

Objective

Bridge the gap between Nintendo's goals and what users are experiencing by conducting usability testings to collect and synthesize insights for design recommendations.

Understanding Nintendo Switch

Nintendo intended for the Switch to be an intuitive and easily usable console for players of all ages and experience levels. Key objectives included encouraging various forms of interaction ranging from sitting on the couch with the console docked to playing via handheld on the go. The design of the hardware — including the touchscreen, Joy-Con configurations, and the dock — was meant to support this flexible and user-centric gaming experience.

Impacts & Outcomes

Research
  • Conducted usability testing with 6 participants across 3 user groups (novice, experienced, parent) to evaluate first-time setup, controller pairing, and parental controls, uncovering critical interaction breakdowns.
  • Synthesized 10+ usability issues into 4 high-priority themes, revealing that unclear system feedback and inconsistent instructions significantly increased cognitive load for novice and parent users.
  • Measured task completion, error rates, time-on-task, and post-task satisfaction across task interactions, identifying where learnability and clarity failed.
Design
  • Translated findings into 6+ prioritized, actionable design recommendations, including clearer onboarding cues, improved feedback states, and simplified parental control flows.
Usability & Iteration
  • Collaborated with 5 other researchers on study design, facilitation, and synthesis, contributing to shared usability insights and evidence-based recommendations.

Process

Dimensions of Interest

Based on user goals and Nintendo's core values, my team evaluated the Switch across seven key dimensions of interest. These served as our framework for testing and analysis:

  • Social Connectivity: Assessed the console's ability to foster community and shared gaming experiences.
  • Accessibility: Evaluated usability for a broad range of users, considering cognitive, physical, and visual needs.
  • Entertainment & Satisfaction: Measured how well the console delivers an enjoyable and engaging experience, from gameplay to content richness.
  • Environmental Sustainability: Focused on the Switch's alignment with environmentally responsible use across its lifecycle.
  • Safety: Looked at features like parental controls and privacy settings that protect users from online risks.
  • Learnability: Assessed how quickly and efficiently new users could understand and begin using the console.
  • Customization: Evaluated the extent to which users can personalize their gaming experience to their unique preferences.

User Groups & Personas

As Nintendo intends their user groups to be diverse across all ages and experiences, we examined the usability of the Nintendo Switch across three user groups: novice, parent, and expert.

  • Novice Users — Individuals who have either never interacted with a Nintendo Switch or have only briefly used one and have never owned the console.
  • Experienced Users — Individuals who have owned a Nintendo Switch for over a year, play at least once a month, and maintain an active Nintendo Online subscription.
  • Parental Users — A subcategory of novice and experienced users, consisting of parents who have never used a Nintendo Switch but have children under 15 who may be prospective users.

Study Design

  • Participants: 6 split into Novice (2), Expert (2), Parent (2)
  • Recruitment & Screener: Discord/social + Qualtrics screener with clear inclusion criteria per group
  • Location & Recording: San Jose State University lab room, camera (Insta360, phone)
  • Role Breakdown: 2–3 Data Collection Observers and Notetakers, 2 Moderators, 1 Technical Supervisor

Metrics

Performance

Task completion time, completion success, and error rate measured across all tasks and user groups.

Subjective

Perception probe questions measuring satisfaction, perceived learnability, ease of use, frustrations, and feature assessments.

Behavioral

Facial and body expressions, non-verbal cues observed and recorded throughout sessions.

Pilot Study

Before starting our actual data collection, we conducted a pilot study to ensure the sessions would run smoothly and to revise any potential issues.

What we looked for: confusing wording, tasks that invite moderator "leading," steps that consistently fail, recording gaps (audio/video), and any room or gear failure points.

Study Flow

1

Introduction (5 min)

Consent form, brief study explanation, participant questions answered.

2

Novice Tasks

Unbox & first-time setup → dock to TV → insert game → pair controller.

3

Expert Tasks

Verify used device condition → add friend remotely → share screenshot → re-download owned title → transfer data → create folders.

4

Parent Tasks

Initial setup for kids → set parental controls → set play-time → set PIN → override restriction.

5

Post-task Questionnaire & Debrief

Asked participants about overall experience and final thoughts. Recorded overall subjective ratings and compensated participants.

Key Findings

There were many findings — below are only the top 3 per category for a more concise overview. To read the full protocol, you can request via wingkoon0718@gmail.com.

First-time setup, parental controls, and social/management flows are the main friction points. Clearer guidance, on-device controls, and small hardware/UI tweaks would remove most novice and parent pain while preserving expert flexibility.

Script-Based
  • First-time setup and docking are confusing: Manuals were perceived as instructions but contained zero setup guidance. On-box instruction visuals lacked clarity and visibility → high cognitive load → users struggled to open, arrange, and dock.
  • Controller pairing lacks clear feedback: Blinking lights and success states were unclear → users could not tell when pairing started or completed.
  • Excessive steps for parental controls: Unnecessary cross-device hopping (console ↔ mobile app) with unclear flow → high memory load and lack of clarity.
UI-Based
  • Data-transfer warning was not salient: The pop-up screen warning "Source console will be deleted" did not command attention and was easily missed → error-prone.
  • Setting states are ambiguous: Grey/orange cues did not convey selection → user hesitation and fear of irreversible changes.
  • Hard to find archived/re-download content: Unclear labels and pathways to owned digital vs. physical titles → users had to recall details rather than recognise them.
Hardware-Based
  • Dock port placement: HDMI/cable placements behind a flap were not obvious to first-time users → increased error rate.
  • Joy-Con usability: Small and hidden release button and lock mechanism → confusion on detachment.
  • Ergonomics edge cases: Physical game card insertion can be difficult for users with limited dexterity or long nails.

Recommendations

We identified high, medium, and low priority recommendations. Below are the top 4. To read the full protocol, request via wingkoon0718@gmail.com.

01

Quick-Start Guide

Include instructions in the manual or add a visual quick-start guide with labeled diagrams for setup.

02

On-Console Parental Controls

Make core parental controls configurable on-console (per-child/per-day rules) to remove cross-device friction.

03

Clearer Warnings & Feedback

Emphasize critical warnings — e.g., data-transfer "delete source" message — and clear pairing success states with "Connected" message or next-step prompt.

04

Dock Port Labels

Expose and label dock ports more clearly: make HDMI path visible, add cable icons/arrows on the dock to reduce TV-setup errors.

Takeaways

This project was my first full end-to-end experience writing a study protocol and executing usability testing.

  • Pilot run: Even a very short pilot is critical — it surfaces "simple" but important oversights like missed setup steps and lets you revise the script and logistics before main sessions.
  • Adapt while moderating: I adjusted probes and timeboxes in-session, used predefined non-leading recovery prompts, and paused to observe breakdowns rather than guiding users through them. This kept data quality high while ensuring participants could progress.
  • Keep scope tight and connect every insight: Instead of trying to accomplish every task, we prioritised depth — which allowed us to uncover more valuable insights and seamlessly tie them together.
