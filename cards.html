Card Understanding — US Market

A comprehensive market research study on the US card landscape — covering credit, debit, co-branded, store, and gift cards. Mapped card types, user motivations, demographics, and business implications to inform product strategy in the payment space.

Role
UX Researcher
Context
Current employer
Scope
6 card types · US market
Tasks
Market Research Competitive Analysis User Perception Analysis Synthesis

Objective

To build a comprehensive understanding of the US card market — covering general credit and debit cards, co-branded variants (credit and debit), store cards, and gift cards — including card penetration, user motivations, demographics, and how each card type serves both users and the brands behind them.

Impacts & Outcomes

  • Mapped 6 card types across description, key benefits, limitations, market size, and typical user profiles — creating a single reference for strategic decisions.
  • Identified three universal adoption drivers across all card types: brand-linked value, tailored rewards, and ecosystem integration.
  • Surfaced distinct user perception profiles (reward-motivated, convenience-driven, brand-affinity) that cut across card types and inform targeting strategy.
  • Analyzed 15+ specific card products across travel, cashback, co-branded credit and debit, store, and gift categories — comparing advantages, limitations, and user fit.
  • Documented payment and repayment flows for credit cards to clarify how authorization, clearing, and settlement work end-to-end.
Process

Research approach

This was a secondary research and synthesis project — I reviewed industry reports, consumer surveys, and financial platform documentation to build a structured picture of the card market from multiple angles: the user's perspective, the brand's perspective, and the broader market context. Sources included Forbes, Federal Reserve, Capital One Shopping Research, YouGov surveys, and financial platform documentation.

I organized findings into a consistent framework across all 6 card types — covering description, key benefits, limitations, user perception, usage patterns, and demographics — to make the research immediately usable for product and strategy teams.

The three adoption drivers

Across all card types, three characteristics consistently drove usage and adoption:

01

Brand-linked value

Instant discounts, cash back, loyalty points, and exclusive perks tied to specific brands or networks drive initial adoption and repeat use.

02

Tailored rewards

Cards that match their reward structure to user spending habits — travel for high earners, debit co-brands for debt-averse users — outperform generic alternatives.

03

Ecosystem integration

Cards that embed into users' routines via mobile apps, loyalty programs, and digital wallets see higher engagement and retention.

Card type overview

Credit Card

Credit Card

  • Why users choose it: Build credit, earn rewards, cover future expenses.
  • Key draw: Rewards (travel or cashback), fraud protection, installment options.
  • Limitation: Requires approval, risk of debt/interest (APR 20–24%).
  • Main networks: Visa, Mastercard, Amex, Discover.
Debit Card

Debit Card

  • Why users choose it: Avoid debt, expense transparency, cash access.
  • Key draw: No credit check, instant fund access, widely accepted.
  • Limitation: Limited rewards, no credit building.
  • Demographics: Gen Z for daily use; older users for security.
Co-branded Credit

Co-branded Credit

  • Why users choose it: Brand-specific rewards while building credit.
  • Key draw: Exclusive perks, easier approval (min. 640 score), loyalty integration.
  • Limitation: Best value only if spending frequently at partner brand; high APR.
  • Demographics: Higher earners; 35% earn $80K+.
Co-branded Debit

Co-branded Debit

  • Why users choose it: Brand rewards without credit risk or approval.
  • Key draw: No credit check, no debt risk, exclusive partner discounts.
  • Limitation: Lower rewards than credit; weak purchase protection.
  • Demographics: Millennials/Gen Z, low-mid income, debt-averse users.
Store Card

Store Card

  • Why users choose it: Instant discounts and savings at a specific retailer.
  • Key draw: Easy approval, higher rewards within that store, store-account integration.
  • Limitation: Only usable at targeted merchant; high APR.
  • Demographics: Frequent shoppers at the targeted store.
Gift Card

Gift Card

  • Why users choose it: Easy gifting, budget control, flexible for recipient.
  • Key draw: No approval needed, instant digital delivery, customizable amounts.
  • Limitation: One-time use, no rewards, fraud risk.
  • Market: US = 36% ($447.7B) of global gift card market in 2025.

Credit card types — travel vs cashback

✈ General & travel

  • Points/miles redeemable for flights, hotels, transfers.
  • Perks: lounge access, hotel credits, TSA PreCheck, no foreign transaction fee.
  • Annual fee typically $95–$695+.
  • Best for: frequent travelers and high spenders.
  • Requires tracking bonus categories and transfer partners.

💰 Cashback

  • Straightforward 1–5% back on purchases.
  • Redeemable as statement credits, bank deposits, or gift cards.
  • Often no annual fee.
  • Best for: everyday spenders who want simple rewards.
  • Minimal management needed.

User perception profiles

Across all card types, four user perception archetypes consistently appeared:

Reward Motivated

Attracted by exclusive rewards, discounts, perks, and access. Will choose the card that maximizes return on their specific spending habits.

Convenience-Driven

Wants simplicity and rewards for frequent shopping places. Values ease of use over maximizing reward value.

Brand-affinity Enthusiast

Seeks emotional connection, exclusive status, and loyalty with a specific brand. The card is part of their identity with that brand.

Deal Seeker / Complementary User

Uses the card for specific occasions or as a secondary card alongside a primary card. Values targeted discounts over breadth of rewards.

Consumer insights

Users don't think in card categories — they think in moments. The right card is the one that rewards them for what they already do.

  • Income shapes card type, not just spending: 98% of households earning $100K+ have a credit card vs. 57% of households earning under $25K — reward programs are structurally more accessible to higher earners.
  • Gen Z is debit-first but reward-hungry: 69% of Gen Z reported weekly/daily co-branded debit card use — they want the benefits of rewards without the risk of credit. Co-branded debit is a direct response to this segment.
  • Co-branded cards require active spending to justify: Their value proposition only lands for users who shop frequently at the partner brand. Infrequent shoppers see lower rewards than a general cashback card.
  • Mobile and digital wallet integration is now table stakes: 45% of credit card transactions are mobile. Cards that don't integrate with Apple Pay or Google Pay lose usage to those that do.
  • Gift cards peak on occasion, not habit: 64% of US consumers buy gift cards as holiday gifts; 56% for special events. They're not part of a loyalty loop — they're a gifting tool.

Business impact

  • For brands launching co-branded cards: Success depends on reward differentiation and active use at the partner brand. Cards with weak partner-spend incentives underperform general cashback alternatives.
  • For debit-first markets: Co-branded debit is an underutilized opportunity — it reaches younger, debt-averse users that co-branded credit misses, without credit approval barriers.
  • Ecosystem lock-in drives long-term value: Cards embedded in loyalty programs, mobile wallets, and digital accounts generate higher engagement and retention than standalone products.
  • Gift cards generate brand revenue beyond direct spend: Unused balance becomes direct profit. Fraud is the primary risk — it remains the most common card fraud type in the US.
  • Installment plan adoption is growing: Over 30 million Americans use installment plans. Offering 0% installment options at checkout reduces cart abandonment and increases average order value, particularly for Gen Z and Millennials.
Takeaways

What I learned

  • Market research reveals the "why" behind product decisions: Understanding that co-branded debit exists specifically for debt-averse Millennials and Gen Z reframes how you think about product positioning — it's not just a debit card with perks, it's a credit-free loyalty play.
  • User perception is more segmented than demographics suggest: The same demographic can hold very different mental models about cards. Mapping perception profiles alongside demographics gave a more actionable picture than age or income alone.
  • Secondary research requires active synthesis, not just aggregation: The value wasn't in collecting facts — it was in finding the patterns across card types that product teams could actually use to make decisions.
